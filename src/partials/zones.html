Five layers of ocean.

Oceanographers divide the water column into five layers. Each is darker than the one above it, and under more pressure. Vesper passes through all five on the way down.

    Epipelagic

    Nearly all of the ocean’s photosynthesis happens in this thin top layer. Its phytoplankton make about half of the oxygen produced on Earth.

    Twilight zone

    200–1,000mFrom 200 to 1,000 metres

    Mesopelagic

    There is too little light here for plants to grow. Every night, lanternfish, krill and squid rise toward the surface to feed, then sink back before dawn: the largest migration on the planet.

    Light
    1% down to a trace

  3. Midnight zone

    1,000–4,000mFrom 1,000 to 4,000 metres

    Bathypelagic

    Sunlight never reaches this far, so most animals here make their own. An anglerfish hangs a glowing lure in front of its jaws, and waits.

    Light
    Living light only
    Water
    4 to 1.5 °C
    Pressure

  4. The abyss

    4,000–6,000mFrom 4,000 to 6,000 metres

    Abyssopelagic

    Flat plains of soft sediment cover most of the deep sea floor. Sea cucumbers, brittle stars and tripod fish live on marine snow, the slow fall of debris from above.

    Light
    Living light only
    Water
    About 1.5 °C
    Pressure
    403 to 604 bar

  5. Hadal zone

    6,000–10,935mFrom 6,000 to 10,935 metres

    Hadalpelagic

    Trenches, where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another. A snailfish filmed at 8,336 metres is the deepest fish ever seen; below it, amphipods scavenge all the way to the bottom.

    Light
    Living light only
    Water
    1.5 to 2.5 °C
    Pressure
    604 to 1,100 bar
